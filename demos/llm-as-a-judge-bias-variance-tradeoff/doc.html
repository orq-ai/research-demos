Bias-Variance Tradeoff in LLM-as-a-Judge: Single Judge, Repetition, and Panels

Author: Bauke Brenninkmeijer Date: 2026-05-22 Status: Draft

TL;DR

The intuition holds, with one important refinement from the recent literature:

Strategy Variance Bias
Single judge, 1 call High (decoding + sampling noise) High (judge-specific systematic biases)
Single judge, repeated / more prompts ↓ Reduced Unchanged — biases do not average out
Panel of diverse judges (PoLL, jury) ↓ Reduced ↓ Reduced — biases partially cancel across families
Balanced round-robin panel (CyclicJudge) ↓ Lowest at fixed budget ↓ Eliminated in expectation

Repetition attacks sampling noise. Only changing the set of judges attacks judge bias. This is the central finding of Zhu et al. (2026) — and it cleanly formalizes the bias-variance framing.

1. Where the bias comes from

A single LLM judge carries systematic, model-specific biases that are well documented:

  • Position bias — Order of candidates flips outcomes. Shi et al. (2025, arXiv:2406.07791) ran ~150k evaluation instances across 15 judges and showed position bias is not random: it varies by judge and task, is only weakly tied to component length, and is strongly modulated by the quality gap between candidates. So mitigations (swapping positions, averaging) work but are judge-dependent.
  • Verbosity bias — Judges prefer longer answers regardless of quality.
  • Self-preference / intra-model bias — Judges favor outputs from their own model family (Zheng et al. 2023; Verga et al. 2024).
  • Reasoning ceiling — Judges are weak on math/logic and inherit their own model’s blind spots (Zheng et al. 2023, arXiv:2306.05685).

These are properties of the judge, not properties of the sample. They don’t shrink with more samples from the same judge — which is exactly what makes them “bias” in the statistical sense.

2. Why repetition alone is insufficient — the formal argument

Zhu et al. (2026, CyclicJudge, arXiv:2603.01865) decompose each evaluation score as

\[X_{ij\ell} = \mu_\theta + \alpha_i + \beta_{ij} + \gamma_\ell + \varepsilon_{ij\ell}\]

with terms for scenario difficulty (\(\alpha\)), generation noise (\(\beta\)), judge bias (\(\gamma\)), and residual noise (\(\varepsilon\)).

The variance of the benchmark mean then decomposes into four terms. Critically:

  • The \(\alpha\), \(\beta\), \(\varepsilon\) terms shrink with more scenarios, more generations, more decoding rolls.
  • The \(\gamma\) term does not shrink unless you change \(K\), the number of distinct judges.

Empirically they show \(V_\gamma\) (the judge-bias variance contribution to the benchmark mean) is the dominant term in typical single-judge, single-generation settings on MT-Bench and MindEval.

This is the cleanest existing evidence for the intuition: repetition of one judge ≈ pure variance reduction, no bias correction.

3. Why panels reduce bias — and how aggregation matters

Two complementary lines of work:

3a. PoLL — Panel of LLM Evaluators (Verga et al., arXiv:2404.18796)

A jury of smaller models from disjoint families (e.g. Claude-family + GPT-family + open-weight) correlates with human preferences better than a single large judge across 6 datasets and 3 settings, while being ~7× cheaper. The mechanism: biases from different families are not perfectly correlated, so simple averaging cancels a substantial fraction.

Caveat: PoLL benefits depend on judge diversity. Three Claude variants ≠ a real panel — biases would be too correlated to cancel.

3b. BT-σ — Judge-reliability-aware aggregation (Qian et al., arXiv:2602.16610)

Naive averaging treats all judges as equally trustworthy. BT-σ extends Bradley-Terry with a per-judge discriminator parameter, learned jointly from pairwise comparisons (no human labels needed). It outperforms naive panel averaging when judges differ in reliability. Useful when you suspect one of your judges is a weak link.

3c. CyclicJudge — Optimal panel design under fixed budget (Zhu et al.)

The core practical contribution: at any fixed judge-call budget, a balanced round-robin assignment of judges to (scenario, generation) cells — every judge used equally often, never twice on the same cell — provably:

  1. Cancels the \(\gamma\) (judge bias) term in expectation,
  2. Achieves lower total variance than both random-single-judge and all-judges-on-everything,
  3. Cuts benchmark score variance by 27–40% vs random single-judge at realistic budgets.

So under a budget constraint, the optimal design is not “more reps of one judge” or “all judges on every item” — it’s balanced rotation.

4. When a single judge is fine

Not every setting needs a panel.

  • Zheng et al. (2023) — GPT-4 alone hits >80% agreement with humans on MT-Bench, comparable to human-human agreement, given prompt-engineered mitigations (position swap, CoT, reference answers).
  • Clinical / rubric-constrained tasks — In a psychosis-support safety eval (OpenReview 78ootwhyDQ), a single judge beat a jury against clinician consensus (Cohen’s κ higher for human×judge than human×jury). When the rubric is tight and the bias surface is small, panel noise can hurt more than it helps.

Rule of thumb: panels matter when the task is subjective, stylistic bias drives outcomes, or cross-provider fairness is at stake (leaderboards). Single judges are fine when the rubric is objective, answers are short/structured, and you only need relative deltas with one judge held fixed.

5. Recommendations for our eval framework

Mapping back to what we run at orq:

  1. For internal iterative dev (prompt tuning, A/B on a single agent): single strong judge + position-swap + deterministic decoding is fine. Cheaper, faster, sufficient for relative signal.
  2. For leaderboards / cross-provider comparison / customer-facing benchmarks: use a panel of ≥3 judges from different families with CyclicJudge-style round-robin assignment. Budget the same number of calls, get lower variance and unbiased ranking.
  3. For pairwise preference tasks: PoLL with majority vote is a strong baseline; upgrade to BT-σ when you suspect judge-quality heterogeneity (e.g. mixing a small open-weight model with a frontier model).
  4. Always report which judge(s) produced the score. The judge identity is now understood to be the dominant source of disagreement across benchmark replications.

6. Open questions

  • Correlation structure of biases across families — How “disjoint” do panel members really need to be? PoLL assumes near-independence; CyclicJudge’s bias-cancellation guarantee weakens as judge biases become correlated. Empirical mapping of cross-family bias correlation is missing.
  • Debate vs vote — NeurIPS 2025 work on multi-agent debate with adaptive stability detection beats majority voting at similar cost. Unclear when debate is worth the latency.
  • Judge drift — If frontier-model judges shift over time (new versions), longitudinal benchmark scores get a moving \(\gamma\). Pinning judge versions or running a rolling calibration set is probably necessary.

Verification of intuition

Claim Supported? Strongest evidence
Repetition reduces variance ✅ Yes Zhu et al. 2026 — α, β, ε terms shrink with more samples
Repetition reduces bias ❌ No Zhu et al. 2026 — γ term independent of N
Panels reduce bias ✅ Yes, conditional on judge diversity Verga et al. 2024 (PoLL), Zhu et al. 2026 (CyclicJudge)
Panels also reduce variance under fixed budget ✅ Yes Zhu et al. 2026 — 27–40% variance reduction vs random single judge

Intuition holds. The non-obvious refinement: balanced round-robin is strictly better than either “average all judges on all items” or “random single judge per item” — and that’s where the budget should go.
