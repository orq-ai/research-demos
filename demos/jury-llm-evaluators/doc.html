When to use LLM judges

LLM-as-a-judge has become the default pattern for evaluating generative outputs at scale. You're producing thousands of completions — question-answering, summarisation, code, agents — and human evaluation gets expensive fast. Judges fill the gap: score or rank outputs in seconds, at a fraction of the cost, with consistent rubrics.

The convenience hides a critical tradeoff. You've replaced human judgement with machine judgement, and machine judgement has its own systematic biases.

One judge, several biases

A single LLM judge is not neutral. Different training data, different architectures, different instruction-tuning, different constitutional constraints — these manifest as measurable, repeatable biases:

  • Verbosity bias. Larger instruction-tuned models reward longer answers even when concise is better.
  • Stylistic bias. Favouring argumentative structures that match the model's own training distribution.
  • Conservative bias. Systematically hedging on controversial / edge-case responses.
  • Confidence mismatch. Models trained to express high confidence penalise "I'm not sure" even when it's correct.
  • Domain brittleness. Smaller models struggle on medical, legal, technical evaluation.
  • Self-preference bias. Judges over-rate outputs from their own family. Yang et al. (2026) quantify this across GPT-4, Claude, Gemini.
  • Position bias. In pairwise A-vs-B, order outranks content. Shi et al. (2025) show position consistency varies wildly across 15 judges × 22 tasks.
  • Length bias. Longer answers win, regardless of correctness. Documented in MT-Bench.

These biases are systematic, not random. Run the same prompt through the same judge twice and you'll get the same skewed score. If your evaluation metric leans one way, your whole pipeline silently selects for that lean.

Pointwise vs pairwise

Two judge protocols dominate the literature. The bias-reduction case for panels depends on which one you're running.

  • Pointwise (direct scoring). Judge sees one response, returns a score. Output is pass/fail, a category, or a 0–100 numeric. Useful for objective tasks where the rubric is writable down: factuality, toxicity, code correctness.
  • Pairwise (A vs B). Judge sees two candidate responses to the same input and picks A, B, or TIE. Useful for subjective tasks where humans can rank but can't articulate a closed rubric. Helpfulness, preference, "which answer is better?"

That gap is exactly why the bias-reduction case for panels is strongest in pairwise mode. For pointwise objective tasks, a single strong model is often enough. For pairwise subjective tasks — which is most of what humans actually want graded — single judges are too noisy, and panels become structural.

More annotators, less bias

Multiple judges solve the bias problem through redundancy. No single judge's biases dominate if others pull in different directions. This is why inter-rater studies and A/B tests work. Diversity of perspective reduces systematic error.

The strongest empirical case for doing this with LLMs is Verga et al. (2024), "Replacing Judges with Juries". Their Panel of LLM evaluators (PoLL) — three small models from different providers (command-r, gpt-3.5-turbo, haiku) — beat a single large judge across six datasets, mitigated the biases, and cost about 7× less.

The math: if three uncorrelated judges agree, joint error is small. If they disagree, you've detected a hard datapoint that probably warrants human review. This mirrors classical ensemble classification — small diverse models beat one large one.

How to choose your judges

This is fundamentally an operations research task: pick the models that reach the human-agreement baseline for the cheapest cost. The "pick the best models" instinct misses the real optimisation goal.

Start by establishing a realistic agreement ceiling. Human annotators don't hit perfect agreement on pairwise judgements — demanding perfection from judge models sets an unrealistic standard that flatters bad evaluation work.

The ceiling is human–human agreement

  • MT-Bench reports human-human pairwise around 0.81 (excluding ties); GPT-4 vs humans at 0.85.
  • Chatbot Arena reports crowd-vs-expert agreement 72–83%, expert-vs-expert 79–90%.
  • Cohen's κ band: 0.6–0.8 = "substantial"; > 0.8 = "near-perfect". With ties and harder prompts, strong human pools land around κ ≈ 0.65.

A short selection protocol

  1. Literature review. Find the most similar task that already published agreement results. In our example, human preference between two answers was about 0.65 κ. That was our baseline target for the 3-model panel.
  2. Get a small human-labeled set. 100 pairwise items, ideally 2+ annotators so you can compute human-human κ as your in-house ceiling.
  3. Score every candidate against it. Per-judge κ vs the human consensus. Drop anything well below the human-human floor (κ < ~0.5 is usually a discard).
  4. Pick 3 from different providers that hit the baseline, ideally with different error profiles. Two judges that agree with humans at 0.7 but agree with each other at 0.95 give you almost no panel benefit. Want some balance — judges that miss on different prompts.
  5. Log per-judge agreement in production traces. The score that matters long-term isn't the aggregate panel score; it's whether each judge is still tracking your reference set three months later.
The practical sweet spot · a panel that lands collectively at human-human κ, with judges that disagree on different prompts. Pushing higher usually means overfitting to a narrower prompt distribution, not getting smarter.

Panel selection as an optimisation problem

The literature is converging on a formal framing: judge selection is a combinatorial optimisation problem. Pick the cheapest subset of candidate judges whose aggregated agreement clears your target κ, subject to a diversity constraint (no two judges from the same provider family).

# Formal problem
minimize:    cost(judges)
subject to:  κ(judges, human) ≥ κ_target    # e.g. 0.65
constraint:  diversity(judges) > threshold  # no two judges
                                            # from same provider

Recent work attacks this from different angles:

  • Tornede et al. (2025) · multi-objective hyperparameter search for judge configs across cost and accuracy.
  • "Don't Always Pick the Highest-Performing Model" (2026) · greedy mutual-information selection. Correlation matters, not just accuracy.
  • Auto-Prompt Ensemble (Li et al., 2025) · dynamic per-judge confidence weighting on top of static selection.

In practice you don't need to solve this perfectly. The ensemble effect kicks in as long as the providers are different. GPT tends to over-hedge. Claude is nuanced but conservative on edge cases. Mistral / Gemini bring their own quirks. When all three judge the same output, a single model's systematic bias gets diluted — and when they disagree, that disagreement is where the interesting edge cases live.

This extends classical ensemble insight. We combine weak learners to reduce both bias (different models emphasise different patterns) and variance (averaging smooths individual prediction noise). With LLM judges the dynamic is the same. Two independent judge calls might both make mistakes, but if their mistakes point in different directions, majority vote or inspection of disagreement becomes a reliable signal. Research on multi-annotator agreement (Krippendorff's α, Fleiss' κ) shows this effect is strongest with 3–5 annotators. More than that and gains plateau while costs scale linearly.

So the "3 judges" rule isn't arbitrary. It's the point where you capture the ensemble benefit (diverse error surfaces) without overpaying for diminishing returns.

Lessons from running this in practice

We tested the pattern on two workloads: pairwise ranking for tournament-style model grading, and pairwise preference collection for RLHF data generation. Different mechanics, but a few lessons keep showing up.

On setup

  • Make TIE a real label. First instinct is to collapse 1:1:1 splits into "no winner" and move on. Don't. TIE tells you something: the judges can't distinguish, or the samples are genuinely ambiguous. Tracking TIE rate is your diagnostic for prompt clarity.
  • Position swap for pairwise. Re-run hard A vs B comparisons as B vs A and average the verdicts. Position bias is a pairwise problem; this is a cheap fix.

On optimisation

  • Self-judge exclusion is critical. If your generator is Claude, your judges must not include Claude. Models prefer their own outputs; including the sibling model breaks independence.
  • Provider diversity > model diversity. Different families (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google) matter more than three OpenAI models. Mix providers first.

On outputs

  • Disagree on hard cases; that's the point. If three judges always agree, you might be over-fitting to a narrow style. Disagreement on edge cases is a signal.
  • Batch API for offline work. If the eval isn't user-facing (nightly reruns, dataset rescoring), use batch pricing. Halves the bill with a 24-hour SLA.

A note on building this for yourself

Start with the minimum viable jury: three judges on a real prompt, pairwise mode. Don't overthink the first run. This will surface real failure modes — position bias favouring certain positions, one provider consistently rating answers higher, TIE behaviour on specific prompt types — that metrics alone won't catch.

Practically:

  • Run judges in parallel (all three API calls concurrently, not sequentially).
  • Cache judge responses on repeated prompts to save cost during iteration.
  • Log disagreement explicitly. Don't lose signal by averaging too early.
  • Monitor per-judge accuracy against a small labeled holdout set.
  • Iterate on provider mix, not prompt engineering. If one judge is lagging, swap it before tweaking instructions.

Example pairwise prompt

TASK: Compare two answers to a user question and pick the better one.

QUESTION: "How do I optimize database queries for a large dataset?"

ANSWER A:
"Use indexing on frequently queried columns, consider denormalization,
and run EXPLAIN ANALYZE to understand query plans. Query complexity
grows with data size, so these optimizations are critical."

ANSWER B:
"Add indexes to your WHERE clause columns. Use LIMIT to reduce results.
Also, caching helps a lot. You should also consider using Redis or
memcached for faster lookups."

INSTRUCTIONS:
- Judge which answer is more helpful, more accurate, better structured
- Consider: correctness, completeness, clarity, practical usefulness
- Be consistent in how you weight these factors
- Output ONLY: "A", "B", or "TIE"

YOUR DECISION: ___

Run the same prompt to each of the three judges in parallel, then aggregate:

  • If 2+ judges pick A → Winner = A
  • If all three pick different options → hard case, flag for human review
  • If 2+ judges pick TIE → the question may be ambiguous, improve the prompt

The disagreement metric tells you where your prompts are unclear.

Glossary

PoLL
Panel of LLM evaluators. Verga et al. 2024 — three small models from three providers beat one large judge.
Cohen's κ
Agreement coefficient between two raters, corrected for chance. 0.6–0.8 substantial · >0.8 near-perfect.
Fleiss' κ
Generalisation of Cohen's κ to N raters.
Bradley-Terry
Paired-comparison MLE. Roll panel verdicts into a leaderboard.
Self-preference bias

Position bias

TIE
Explicit panel-split label. Don't collapse into null.
